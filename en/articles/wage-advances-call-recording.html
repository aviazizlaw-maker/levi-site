Wage advances and a call recording as evidence: an employee sued for back pay and ended up owing money

  • Court: Tel Aviv Regional Labor Court
  • Case: Labor Case 35507-11-23
  • Decided 26.10.2026
  • Published: November 2, 2026

A metalworker employed for about three months sued for unpaid wages, and the employer counterclaimed for advances paid but never deducted. The employee's claim was dismissed and he was ordered to repay ILS 6,845 and pay costs. The court also explained when a call recording counts for nothing.

What happened

The employee worked as a metalworker for a small business from August to November 2023. His agreed pay was ILS 10,000 net for the first month and ILS 12,500 from the second. Wages were paid by bank transfer and also by cash withdrawals from the employer's account at an ATM. In November, after a pre-dismissal hearing, he was let go and sued the next day. The employer counterclaimed to recover overpayments.

Why the employee's claim failed

The court found that the employee's affidavit lacked an orderly factual account and any calculation: it did not say which period was unpaid or how the shortfall was computed, and the amount claimed dropped between the statement of claim and the affidavit without explanation. In testimony he confirmed receiving ILS 34,250, as the employer said. New allegations raised only under cross-examination, for example that the employer "kept him at home", were rejected as a belated version.

The recording that did not count

The employee submitted a transcript of a call in which, he said, the employer admitted the debt. The court restated the rule: to rely on a recording, a party must submit the recording itself, with an affidavit from whoever recorded it stating the device, date, participants and how the file was kept. A transcript alone was given no weight.

Moreover, the employee admitted the call took place after he filed suit. The court found it reflected an offer to settle, not an admission of debt.

The counterclaim: advances and days not worked

WhatsApp messages, whose content the employee confirmed, showed he asked for and received advances from his second day on the job. Because of a bookkeeping error, they were never deducted on his payslips. An advance left undeducted by mistake can be recovered: ILS 2,719.

It was also proven that he was often absent for personal reasons, which he partly admitted. For days recorded as worked but not actually worked, he must repay ILS 4,126. Even with a global monthly salary, an employer may deduct the share of a month not worked.

Holiday pay, vacation, sick pay and work-injury days were not refunded, however. The employer paid them knowingly, for example sick pay without asking for a certificate, and did not prove a mistake. Such a payment is a benefit that cannot be clawed back later.

The outcome

The main claim was dismissed. The employee must repay ILS 6,845 with interest and pay ILS 7,000 in costs.

Key takeaways

  • Employees: a back-pay claim needs a calculation and references to payslips and bank statements. A general complaint is not enough.
  • Recording a call? Keep the original file. The court will want the recording and a detailed affidavit, not just a transcript.
  • Offering to pay to end a dispute is not necessarily an admission of debt.
  • Employers: document every advance in writing and deduct it on the payslip. A proven error can be corrected; a payment made knowingly is a benefit.

This article summarizes a published Israeli court decision in plain language and is not legal advice. Every case depends on its facts. The parties' names have been omitted. This is a first-instance decision and may be appealed.
