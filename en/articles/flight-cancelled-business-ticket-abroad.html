Flight cancelled during the war: passenger who rushed to buy a business-class ticket gets no refund

  • Court: Krayot Small Claims Court
  • Case: Small Claim 84339-05-26
  • Decided 04.11.2026
  • Published: November 2, 2026

A passenger whose return flight from Bangkok was cancelled when the airspace closed bought a business-class ticket himself for USD 3,300, about two hours after the cancellation notice, and sued the airline for about ILS 13,400. The court dismissed the claim in full, holding that he acted in haste and failed to mitigate his loss, but did not order him to pay costs.

What happened

A passenger who travelled to the Far East to mark his retirement bought, through a travel agency, a round-trip ticket between Tel Aviv and Bangkok from an Israeli airline. The economy-class return flight was set for 18.3.2026, and the return leg was worth USD 388.

In late February and early March 2026, when Operation "Roaring Lion" broke out, Israel's airspace was closed and Israeli airlines operated only limited rescue flights. On 10.3.2026 the airline first notified the passenger of a change to his flight, and later that day of its cancellation. The notice said the flight component would be fully credited and included a link to buy a ticket on a rescue flight, with the option of contacting customer service to get the difference back.

Just two hours after the cancellation notice, the passenger went to the airline's website and bought a business-class ticket for the same date, 18.3.2026, for USD 3,300 (about ILS 10,400). Two days later, on 12.3.2026, the airline told him he had been rebooked free of charge on a rescue flight on 20.3.2026. The passenger flew home on the ticket he had bought and did not show up for the replacement flight. The airline later refunded him, through the travel agency, the value of the original return leg, USD 388, less the agent's commission.

The parties' arguments

The passenger claimed about ILS 13,400: the price of the business-class ticket, plus about ILS 3,000 for distress, trouble and dismissive treatment by customer service staff. He said he had followed the airline's notice, there were no economy seats on 18.3, and the cheaper option was only on 28.3, which with ten extra days abroad would have cost more. He added that he had family and work commitments requiring him to return on time, and that the extra booking created a "double flight".

The airline argued that the cancellation was due to force majeure, that the passenger bought the expensive ticket on his own initiative and in a hurry, eight days before the flight, and that the screenshots he himself submitted showed cheaper economy flights. In its view, it has no duty to fund an upgrade to business class, and the distress claim is barred by the exclusive-cause-of-action rule.

The decision

The court held that closing the airspace in wartime is a special circumstance beyond the airline's control, so the airline is exempt from the fixed statutory compensation under sections 6(a)(3) and 6(e) of the Aviation Services Law (Compensation and Assistance for Flight Cancellation or Change of Conditions).

On the business-class ticket, the court found that the airline's notice allowed passengers to buy a ticket comparable to the original one, not any ticket they chose in order to fly on the original date. The law does not require an airline to pay for an upgrade during an emergency. Under section 14(a) of the Contracts (Remedies for Breach of Contract) Law, an injured party must mitigate its loss, and the passenger did not: he did not wait even a day for rebooking, did not contact the travel agency and did not look into alternatives. What is more, his own screenshots showed economy flights on 13.3 (USD 1,849) and 15.3 (USD 1,749), earlier and with no accommodation costs, and he could have asked the airline for the difference. He also offered no evidence of urgent commitments or accommodation costs, and his ten-day accommodation calculation was found artificial, since a free flight only two days later was available to him.

On distress, the court explained that under section 10 of the Air Transport Law, damage flowing from the cancellation itself does not support a separate claim, although in exceptional cases of separate misconduct by the airline additional compensation may be considered. No rude treatment was found here. The court noted that there was no justification for the airline's failure to answer the passenger's messages, but it was working under an unprecedented load, it refunded the return leg and it also bore the cost of the seat on the replacement flight.

The outcome

The claim was dismissed in full. Although the losing party normally pays costs, the court took into account the stress and uncertainty the passenger faced abroad during the war and, as a matter of leniency, ordered each side to bear its own costs. A request for leave to appeal may be filed with the Haifa District Court within 30 days.

Key takeaways

  • Flight cancelled? Before buying a new ticket, wait for the airline to rebook you, or at least contact the airline and your travel agent.
  • If the airline offers to refund the difference on a ticket you buy, choose one comparable to your original. An upgrade to a pricier class may not be refunded.
  • Check every alternative, including earlier flights. The court will ask whether you kept your loss down.
  • Claiming urgent commitments or accommodation costs? Keep documents and receipts to back them up.
  • A cancellation caused by war and closed skies usually exempts the airline from the fixed statutory compensation, but not from refunding your ticket.
