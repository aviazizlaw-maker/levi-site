An HVAC technician is not a cleaning worker: the limits of the cleaning-sector extension order

  • Court: Beersheba Regional Labor Court
  • Case: Labor Case 16619-01-23
  • Decided 29.10.2026
  • Published: November 2, 2026

A refrigeration and air-conditioning technician who worked 16 years at Dead Sea hotels sued for about ILS 337,000, mostly under the cleaning-sector extension order. The court held the order did not apply and awarded only pension shortfalls and compensation for a missing written notice of employment terms.

What happened

The employee worked from 2006 to 2022 for a company that installs and repairs refrigeration and air-conditioning systems, mainly for hotels at the Dead Sea, at ILS 50 an hour. Dismissed after a hearing, he claimed ILS 336,810. Most of it rested on the 2014 cleaning-sector extension order, which defines an employer as a company "engaged in cleaning and/or maintenance". On that basis he sought seniority pay, refund of deducted breaks, a study fund, pension and severance top-ups and more.

How to tell whether an extension order applies

The court explained that both the employer and the employee are examined, under three tests: the wording of the collective agreement, the official classification of economic sectors, and above all what the employer actually does. The burden of proof is on the employee who relies on the order.

The court adopted the view that "maintenance" in the order must be read in the context of the cleaning sector, not as an opening for any maintenance work at all. In support, the parties to the collective agreement removed the word "maintenance" in 2021 and stated that it governs the cleaning sector.

What the evidence showed

The company provides professional service for mechanical systems: chillers, compressors and pumps. The hotel has its own maintenance staff of about 20. The employee described dismantling and overhauling pumps, studied refrigeration engineering and even opened his own business in the same field. The pipe painting he relied on was technical marking to tell hot water from cold, and degreasing was part of the repair work. His wage was almost double the cleaning-sector rate.

As for documents where the word "maintenance" appeared in the company's name, the court held that the content of the work matters, not the label. All claims based on the order were dismissed.

What was awarded

  • Pension shortfall: the employer admitted contributing 5% instead of 6.5% under the mandatory pension order. ILS 9,144 was awarded, with linkage.
  • Notice of employment terms: the employer never gave the written notice required by law since 2002. Because the employee knew his terms, compensation was set at ILS 2,000.

What was rejected

  • Deducted break hour: it was shown that the employee actually took breaks, rested and ate in his own room, and never objected to the deduction over the years.
  • Pre-dismissal hearing: he received a detailed summons, attended, made his case and sent a written response the next day, and the manager even offered to meet and make peace. The court found the hearing was genuine.
  • Employer's set-off: the employer sought to offset vacation and convalescence pay, claiming he worked only 71.5% of a full-time job. This was not proven, and the payments had been made regularly for years without reservation.

Each side bears its own costs.

Key takeaways

  • Whether an extension order applies depends on what the employer and employee actually do, not on a word in a logo or title.
  • Employers: give every employee written notice of employment terms within 30 days of starting. It has been mandatory since 2002.
  • Check from time to time that pension contribution rates are up to date.
  • A fair pre-dismissal hearing means a detailed summons, a real chance to respond, and an option to reply in writing.
  • An employer seeking to offset overpayments must prove a mistake and calculate precisely. Payments made regularly for years are hard to claw back.
